Horizons for Sovereign AI

1 AI horizons

At universities, “our own AI infrastructure” encompasses very different plans and ambitions, from running an open-weight model for administration to training research models of their own. These differ by orders of magnitude in cost, staffing and feasibility. This report groups them into six horizons and assesses, for each one, how far a German university is from achieving it on a sovereign basis: without dependence on US providers and with its own data kept within its own organisation.

Horizon 1: Agentic computer use in everyday work.

Language models act as agents to carry out everyday knowledge work: summarising documents, writing scripts, correcting and reformatting LaTeX, translating, searching document databases (RAG), and acting as tutors to review school-level and foundational material. Status: achievable today. This horizon should be implemented without delay. A university’s default AI must be a model that minimises data collection and is hosted in the EU or in its own data centre (Section 2.3). In October 2026, models such as GLM-5.3-Flash reached the level of the May 2026 frontier model Claude Opus 4.8 on coding and agentic tasks (Section 4.3).

Horizon 2: Autoformalisation, process automation.

Tasks such as translating informal mathematics into Lean or carrying out longer development and programming tasks are achievable in principle, but may prove less smooth than expected. The requirements are comparable to Horizon 1, but these models sometimes need to run for a long time to incorporate (automated) correction loops into processes.

Horizon 3: Mathematical research, finding proofs.

Frontier AI finds proofs of open questions or contributes to new results. Status: not achievable on a sovereign basis. No open-weight model matches Claude Fable 5.2 or GPT-6.2 Astra. The best, Kimi K3.1 Pro, cannot be hosted for large numbers of users at universities in Germany, but its services can be purchased within the EU (Section 4). In the longer term, consortia could cooperate with commercial providers or operate data centres through public–private partnerships that would also make this hosting possible.

Horizon 4: In-house training.

Training models of one’s own, in three stages: fine-tuning and distillation of open weights, in-house pretraining, and a frontier training run. Status: feasible for very small models. Even training very small models is an extremely demanding undertaking. For example, NVIDIA employs around 500 people and uses an unknown amount of hardware/electricity to train a model suitable for Horizon 1 itself. Training frontier models is unrealistic for research institutions (Section 5).

Horizon 5: New model architectures.

Architectures of one’s own that are better suited to one’s research. Status: unrealistic. Finding a better architecture requires very many training runs, and thus compute time and staff on the scale of Horizon 4. For mathematics, the SAIR community nevertheless discusses architecture search and data-mixture search.

Horizon 6: Custom specialised hardware / New AI approaches.

Hardware of one’s own for new architectures, such as neurosymbolic AI or other novel approaches that are not LLM-based. Status: unclear. There are very many questions. Even if a research breakthrough were to occur at a German university, development, commercialisation and funding are often not possible here, and so on. There is unlimited scope for wishful thinking here.

2 Cross-cutting issues

2.1 Hardware

GPU hardware is expensive, scarcely available in 2026, and depreciates rapidly. It therefore needs to be fully utilised around the clock. An 8-GPU node costs several hundred thousand euros. Operation can be purchased from European providers on a per-token basis. A node of one’s own is only cheaper at high utilisation (Section 4.5). Self-hosting is generally not cheaper; it buys data protection at a premium. This is also borne out by the discussion in the SAIR community, where a mathematics department asked about local models for the same reasons (data protection, independence, lack of funds, existing hardware).

2.2 Staffing

Running large models and developing agent software (Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode) requires specialists whose industry salaries are several times the pay for an E11 post. Even professional computing centres such as the GWDG do not yet operate the largest open-weight models. Universities should join existing open-source projects. In doing so, they build expertise and stay engaged in a development whose pace is set by others.

2.3 Data protection and politics

There are two realities of AI use at universities. Legally, personal data generated at universities (such as submitted examination papers, applications and examination records) may be processed even by EU cloud providers only subject to conditions: under a data processing agreement pursuant to Article 28 GDPR. In practice, however, we observe that such data are often processed through accounts with US providers. Under the CLOUD Act, these providers must disclose data to US authorities when ordered to do so, even if the data are stored outside the USA. Internal documents, policies and senate reports thus combine to form a fairly complete picture of university operations in chat logs. Regardless of the providers’ intentions, this creates a political dependence: universities can neither control nor rule out what may be done with these data. This establishes the priority of Horizon 1.

3 Conclusion

Horizons 1 and 2 should be tackled now, and this is already happening at several levels. Computing centres such as the GWDG operate open-weight models for research. Even if they are not yet running the largest models, this is the right approach, because it builds operational experience and independence. At European level, the AI Factories—HammerHAI and JAIF in Germany—provide compute time for research. The AI Gigafactories are also the right approach, provided that some of the compute time guaranteed there is allocated to research (Section 6). Locally, networking is crucial: universities, computing centres and subject communities should share hardware, models, agent setups and experience, rather than each starting on their own. Open initiatives such as SAIR, whose first phase covers precisely Horizons 1 and 2, provide a framework for this beyond one’s own university.

A product that everyone uses has yet to emerge: many users already have a subscription with a US provider, and IT departments are at capacity. These services do not need to compete with the frontier providers’ products. The aim is to build expertise of one’s own, particularly in working with coding agents (Section 4.4): to understand what is actually happening in this field and to base decisions on facts rather than on the idea that one can simply build one’s own frontier AI.

In what follows, we examine specific aspects in more detail. At the end of the document there is a glossary explaining terms such as “MoE” and others.

4 Background: Model Sizes

“B” stands for English billion, meaning billions of parameters, and “T” for trillion, meaning trillions. Parameters are the learned numbers (weights) of a model.

In a dense model, all parameters enter into the computation of every token. By contrast, almost all large models are mixture-of-experts models (MoE). Their parameters are distributed across many subnetworks (experts), and only some of these do the computation for each token. This is why two figures are given for such models.

  • The total number of parameters determines the memory requirement. All weights must be held in GPU memory because different experts are needed for each token.
  • The active parameters determine the computational effort per token and hence speed, electricity consumption and training costs.

The notation 120B-A12B means 120 billion parameters in total, of which 12 billion are active. GLM-5.3-Flash (320B total, 18B active) therefore needs the memory of a very large model, but performs only as much computation per token as a dense model with 18B. A rule of thumb for memory: 4-bit quantisation needs about 0.5 GB per billion parameters, 8-bit quantisation 1 GB per billion parameters. Memory for each user’s context comes on top of this.

4.1 Small Models for Individual Users

The models for smartphones and laptops are dense. Total and active parameter counts are therefore the same. MoE models start in the desktop class.

  • Smartphone: approx. 2–4B, e.g. the smallest models in the Gemma 4 and Qwen3.5 families.
  • Laptop: approx. 27B, e.g. Qwen3.8-27B. Quantised to 4 bits, it fits on a graphics card with 24 GB; on a laptop, approx. 32 GB RAM is needed.
  • Desktop with 128 GB RAM: approx. 120B, e.g. Nemotron 3 Super 120B-A12B, which is largely open, including much of the training data and the scripts (Section 5; licensed under the NVIDIA Open Model License or OpenMDW). A data point from the SAIR community: a DGX Spark (128 GB unified memory, 273 GB/s, about 4 000–5 000 USD) running Qwen 3.8 Flash Next (125B total, 6B active, 111 GB at 4 bits) solves Hartshorne-level exercises, but not research questions in algebraic geometry.

These models hallucinate noticeably and are mainly suitable for clearly delimited tasks. With a good agent harness, they can nevertheless be used productively: “Read this email and create the corresponding calendar entry” works reliably; “Create a synopsis of these 80 applications” succeeds with a coding agent and suitable SKILL.md files.

4.2 Workstation and Data Centre Class

This requires several GPUs with their own HBM memory, connected by NVLink. For individual users, a computer with a very large amount of unified memory is sometimes sufficient.

These models are sufficient for a great many uses. At launch, Kimi K3 ranked behind only Fable and GPT-5.6 Sol in benchmarks. The current frontier (Claude Fable 5.2, GPT-6.2 Astra, Gemini 4 Carbon, as of 17. 11. 2026) is one generation ahead. Since 23. 10. 2026, there has been a successor, Kimi K3.1 Pro (2.8T total, 104B active), also with open weights. The weights of all the models mentioned are freely downloadable, but their licences differ (MIT, Apache 2.0, and a bespoke licence for Kimi K3). Running them is expensive: Kimi K3 needs 1.4 TB of GPU memory for its weights alone, plus the KV cache for each user’s context, which can require more memory than the weights with long contexts (1M tokens) and many concurrent users. The sizes of frontier models are not public; according to unconfirmed reports, Fable has about 10T parameters, and Astra more.

4.3 Performance Comparison

There are a great many benchmarks for language models, covering knowledge, mathematics, programming and agentic tasks, and new models appear every week. Artificial Analysis (https://artificialanalysis.ai) provides a good overview: the site measures open and closed models using the same benchmarks and presents performance, price and speed side by side.

Table 2 compares open and closed models using measurements by Artificial Analysis, collected independently of the developers. These are benchmark results, hence reported rather than verified through our own use. The Intelligence Index combines ten evaluations, four of them agentic; Terminal-Bench 4.0 and GDPval-AA are purely agentic. In this measurement, GLM-5.3-Flash is on a par with Claude Opus 4.8 and usually ahead in the agentic benchmarks. The strongest open model at the time, GLM-5.3, was around 13 index points behind the best model at the time, Claude Opus 5.5.

Table 2 reflects the position on 16. 10. 2026. Since then, Artificial Analysis has revised the composition of the index (version 5.0.1, nine evaluations, six involving tool use). The new scores are not comparable with the older ones, and Claude Opus 4.8 is no longer evaluated. As of 17. 11. 2026, Claude Fable 5.2 leads with 65 points, ahead of GPT-6.2 Astra (62), Gemini 4 Carbon (60) and Claude Opus 5.5 (59). The strongest open model is Kimi K3.1 Pro with 46 points; GLM-5.3 scores 32 and GLM-5.3-Flash 28. Rankings of this kind change within weeks. Any assessment of a model must therefore be read together with its date.

4.4 Agents and Correction Loops

Benchmarks primarily measure the model. In practice, however, what matters is the overall system of model, harness and tools. Many weaknesses of models, above all hallucinations, are addressed in practice not by better models but by correction loops: a coding agent executes code, runs tests, reads error messages and corrects itself. In mathematics, the Lean kernel plays this role. An open model in a good agent therefore achieves considerably more than its benchmark scores might suggest. Studying coding agents, their harnesses and ways of working is foundational work and at least as important as choosing the model.

4.5 Self-hosting or Buying Access: a Rough Calculation

Is it worth having your own GPU node rather than paying per token? We work this out for GLM-5.3-Flash, the model from Horizon 1. All inputs are reported rather than measured by us, and date from November 2026.

Own node. The weights at 8 bits occupy 306 GiB and therefore do not fit on two H200s (282 GB combined). The standard recipe for the serving software vLLM calls for eight H200s. According to the recipe, the 4-bit version requires the newer Blackwell GPU generation. A server with eight H200s costs around 318 000 USD net and draws 9.1 kW. Depreciated over four years, that is around 80 000 USD per year. Electricity adds around 16 000 EUR (PUE 1.2 and 17.2 ct/kWh), for a combined total just under 100 000 per year. At this level of precision, we do not distinguish between USD and EUR. Staff, space, networking and outages are not included.

Throughput. A published measurement on this hardware (eight H200s, vLLM, requests with 8 192 input tokens and 256 output tokens) gives, at 128 concurrent requests, 2 359 output tokens and 9.3 requests per second. At full utilisation around the clock, the node would therefore process 2.4 trillion input tokens and 74 billion output tokens per year.

Buying access. Cortecs, an intermediary for providers based in the EU, charges, for GLM-5.3-Flash, 0.09 EUR per million input tokens and 0.314 EUR per million output tokens. The stated volume of tokens costs around 240 000 EUR there. At the US intermediary OpenRouter, prices across the 44 endpoints for this model range from 0.04 and 0.08 USD to 0.23 and 0.75 USD per million input and output tokens. The developer Z.ai charges 0.15 and 0.50 USD; the only provider listed there as European charges 0.175 and 0.49 USD.

Result. At full utilisation, purchased tokens cost about two and a half times the hardware and electricity costs of the university’s own node. The university’s own node is therefore cheaper from about 40 per cent continuous utilisation. If one staff post for operation is added (assumption: 90 000 EUR per year), the threshold rises to just under 80 per cent. A working week of 40 to 50 hours amounts to 24 to 30 per cent of the week. An individual university is therefore unlikely to reach the threshold; a consortium or a data centre serving many institutions is more likely to do so. The calculation is rough. It applies only to the stated load profile, and does not account for prompt caching, which reduces the price when buying access and the load on a university’s own node.

5 Training Costs

This section supports Horizon 4 with publicly available figures. For frontier models, costs and computational effort are almost invariably secret. In brief: the final training run for a model up to the 100B-MoE class (“desktop”) costs millions. The major items are staff, data work and access to hardware.1

Training involves several stages: data is first collected, filtered and tested in smaller ablation runs, followed by pretraining on trillions of tokens as the most computationally expensive step. Next come mid- and long-context training, followed by post-training consisting of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning, which teaches capabilities such as reasoning and tool use.

5.1 Disclosed Figures

Few developers disclose monetary amounts. DeepSeek put the final run of DeepSeek-V3 at 2.788 million H800-hours, or 5.6 million USD at 2 USD per GPU-hour, explicitly excluding preliminary experiments and hardware purchases. Ai2’s figures for Olmo 3 32B were 1.38 million H100-hours and 2.75 million USD. Meta reports GPU-hours, but no monetary amounts. Alibaba discloses token counts for Qwen (i.e. tokens processed during training: 18T for Qwen2.5, 36T for Qwen3), but has not disclosed GPU type, GPU count or costs for any generation. The largest model in the next generation, Qwen4-Max, has been offered through Alibaba’s API since November 2026, so far without open weights (reported, as of 17. 11. 2026). NVIDIA reports neither GPU-hours nor costs for Nemotron 3. Table 3 summarises the figures, distinguishing what the amounts include: most represent the rental value of GPU-hours for the final run, some are estimates by Epoch AI, and others are total budgets for entire programmes. Estimated GPU-hours are derived from the formula C = 6NT (computational effort in FLOP equals six times active parameters times tokens).

These figures are unsuitable for budget planning, but useful for orders of magnitude and comparisons. The rental values are lower bounds: they include neither the preceding experiments, for which the worked example below allows a further 1.5 to 2 times the final run, nor data work and staff, which according to Epoch AI account for 29–49 per cent of the total cost of frontier models. OpenGPT-X shows how large the gap can be (Section 5.3): 14 million EUR in funding for a 7B model whose final run has a calculated rental value of less than 1 million EUR.

Three observations. First, an open run (2.75–5.6 million USD) and a frontier run (100–500 million USD) differ by a factor of about 100. Second, nobody starts from scratch: NVIDIA’s “fully open” Nemotron data was synthetically augmented using Qwen and DeepSeek models, and NVIDIA’s documentation puts the released pretraining data at 40 to 50 per cent of the internal mix. Qwen3 provides the most striking figure on the cost of starting afresh: reasoning post-training for Qwen3-8B took 17 920 GPU-hours using reinforcement learning, but only 1 800 GPU-hours using distillation from the larger model. The calculated pretraining requirement for the same 8B model is 1.25 million GPU-hours. For the RL stage of DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek reports around 294 000 USD. Third, an open run of this class can also be organised outside the major companies. The Marin project (Section 6) completed, on 5. 11. 2026, the pretraining of an MoE model with 535B parameters, of which 23B are active, on 18T tokens. The 768 GB200 GPUs were provided by a foundation. The project puts the computational effort at around 2.6 · 1024 FLOP. Under the assumptions of the following worked example, this corresponds to about 2.4 million H100-hours or a rental value of 7 million EUR.

5.2 Worked Example at Scales Relevant to Universities

Table 4 calculates training runs at scales that could be considered for a German consortium. Assumptions: C = 6NT with N denoting active parameters; H100 with 989 TFLOP/s peak performance; utilisation (MFU) of 40 per cent for dense models (Llama 3 achieved 38–43 per cent) and 30 per cent for MoE; rental at 3 EUR per H100-hour (Scaleway 2.87 EUR, OVHcloud 3.33 EUR, as of autumn 2026); energy of 1.28 kWh per H100-hour including node, networking and cooling at PUE 1.2; electricity price 17.2 ct/kWh (BDEW 2026, medium-sized businesses). Ablations, restarts, post-training, data preparation and staff are not included; these require an additional budget of 1.5 to 2 times the final run.

A plausibility check against measured values gives good results: OLMo 2 7B measured 157 MWh for 4T tokens; Meta reported 30.8 million H100-hours for Llama 3.1 405B, including restarts and long-context phases.

Translated to JUPITER: a single GCS Large-Scale project at the threshold of 1.73 million GPU-hours per year covers the 30B/10T or 120B-MoE/15T example. A dense 400B run would, at 25 million GPU-hours, account for almost the entire allocation of a GCS call offering 27.3 million GPU-hours (as of July 2026), for which GCS expects around 90 applications. The realistic pretraining ceiling for a consortium is therefore 10–30B dense models or approximately 120B-A12B MoE models per year. The Apertus class (70B on 15T tokens, 6–9 million GPU-hours) was possible in Switzerland only because 40 per cent of an exascale system was reserved for one quarter.

5.3 Data, Electricity, Staff

Data. The raw corpora are freely available: FineWeb (15T tokens), DCLM (4T), Nemotron-Pretraining-Dataset (6.6T, of which around 3T is synthetic), The Stack v2 (900B tokens of code). Iteration is expensive: the ablations for FineWeb alone took 80 000 H100-hours, and 1T tokens of synthetic data generated through an API currently cost 0.75 to 1.5 million USD. German text is two orders of magnitude scarcer than English text: FineWeb-2 contains 262 billion German words, and the licensing-compliant “German Commons” 155 billion tokens.

Law. Universities are in a favourable legal position. § 60d UrhG permits research organisations to carry out text and data mining for scientific research. The law provides for rights holders to reserve their rights only under the general exception in § 44b UrhG, not under § 60d. In the LAION proceedings on 10. 12. 2025, the OLG Hamburg ruled that non-commercial research organisations can rely on § 60d and allowed an appeal to the BGH. However, in the GEMA v OpenAI proceedings (11. 11. 2025), the LG München I classified the memorisation of song lyrics in model weights as reproduction. This judgment is not final. Among other things, the AI Act requires providers of general-purpose models to have a policy for complying with copyright law and to publish a summary of training content (Art. 53). The Commission’s guidelines give a training computation of more than 1023 FLOP as an indicator of such a model. Even the 7B/4T example in Table 4 exceeds this value. The obligations apply as soon as a model is placed on the market in the EU, which the guidelines also take to include uploading to a public repository. Research and development before this step are exempt, and some documentation obligations do not apply to models under a free licence.

Tasks Rather Than Texts. For pretraining, the pool of freely available texts described above exists; for post-training, there is nothing comparable. Open Athena puts it this way: pretraining consumes documents, post-training consumes tasks, meaning an environment, a goal, tools and automatic evaluation.2 Even software development, which records much of its work through version control and tests, yields little: for the SWE-bench benchmark, just over 90 000 pull requests yielded 2 294 tasks. OpenAI then had 93 experienced Python developers manually review 1 699 of these tasks. More than two thirds were flagged, and 500 ultimately formed SWE-bench Verified. The major companies therefore pay experts to translate their work into such tasks. Two implications follow for universities. How experts proceed in a concrete situation is a valuable asset. It arises incidentally when working with agents and accrues to the provider whose service is used. And mathematics may be in a more favourable position than other disciplines, because a formalised statement with the Lean kernel as verifier is already a task with automatic evaluation. Whether the existing formal libraries are sufficient for this remains an open question.

Electricity. In Germany, the energy for one H100-hour costs 0.19 to 0.22 EUR; in Finland, a third of that. This is around 10 per cent of the rental cost, but for a university with allocated computing time, it is the only item paid from its budget: 13 000 EUR for a 7B model, 280 000 EUR for a 30B model. The Energy Efficiency Act covers data centres with a connected load of 300 kW or more. Those starting operations from July 2026 must achieve a PUE of no more than 1.2 and reuse at least 10 per cent of the energy as waste heat.

Staff. What the tables do not show is usually larger than what they do show. OpenGPT-X received around 14 million EUR for ten partners over three years and delivered a 7B model whose training run alone has a calculated rental value of less than 1 million EUR. Apertus had a team of over 100 people, Nemotron around 500 full-time equivalents, and Marin (Section 6) ten full-time engineers. Epoch AI puts staff at 29–49 per cent of the total cost of a frontier model, and energy at 2–6 per cent. On quality: Teuken-7B scores, across 21 EU languages, 0.543 compared with 0.563 for Llama-3.1-8B; Apertus-70B scores 67.5 per cent compared with 69.8 per cent for Qwen2.5-72B. European public models thus come close to the level of older open models.

5.4 Three Levels

For Horizon 4, the figures suggest a tiered approach:

  1. Fine-tuning and Distillation of Open Weights (Qwen, Nemotron base checkpoints, Apertus): thousands of GPU-hours, within reach of any university with a GPU node. This is the computational side of Horizons 1 and 2 and the realistic core of the SAIR initiative.
  2. Independent Pretraining in the 7–30B Range on open corpora under § 60d UrhG: 0.1 to 1.3 million H100-hours and 13 000 to 280 000 EUR for electricity, fundable through GCS or EuroHPC. The result is a laptop-class model (Section 4.1) at the level of one to two years ago.
  3. Frontier Run : 25 million GPU-hours, 32 GWh, 5–6 million EUR for electricity, plus a team of 50–100 people over several years. The bottleneck is this team rather than computing time. In Germany, this is conceivable only as a national or European undertaking. US companies spend 100–500 million USD per run.

The return from level 2 is expertise, not the model. Open laptop-class models, such as Qwen3.8-27B, are available to download from Hugging Face and are ahead of a first independent run. The electricity cost difference of a factor of 2–3 compared with Scandinavia is a question of location that needs to be acknowledged. Level 3 should not be promised. Building on level 1, it is possible to investigate whether independent post-training improves open models in mathematics. This means fine-tuning on examples and reinforcement learning in which the model learns from automatically evaluated tasks (Section 5.3). Here, the bottleneck is less computing time than the tasks and the people who prepare them.

6 Projects and Initiatives

As of 16. 10. 2026, grouped by role, without claiming to be exhaustive. The entries on Marin, Apertus, OpenEuroLLM and the AI Gigafactories were updated on 17. 11. 2026.

6.1 Open-Weight Models for Mathematics

SAIR Open Math Model Initiative.

Foundation for Science and AI Research, founded in 2025 by Terence Tao with private donors; the competitions are sponsored by XTX Markets and Susquehanna. Announced on 18. 09. 2026:3 open models and open tooling for mathematics. The first phase consists of everyday tools (understanding arguments, checking references, exploring examples, writing code, formalising proofs); it corresponds exactly to Horizons 1 and 2. The aim is the community’s independence from the large AI companies, reproducibility and governance, rather than geographical sovereignty. Partners, plans, model sizes and budget have not yet been specified (as of 10. 10. 2026). The licence remains open: the announcement mentions Apache/MIT/CC BY, while the community has proposed AGPLv3 and CC BY-SA so that AI companies cannot incorporate community work without giving anything in return. Those offering support include Hugging Face (training expertise, data, compute; the organisation behind FineWeb and SmolLM), the Linux Foundation’s Open Model Initiative and Open Athena.

Open Athena and Marin (Jeff Hammerbacher).

A US non-profit (https://openathena.ai) that builds foundation models with academic laboratories, employs engineers, brokers access to compute and coordinates cooperation across universities. Its largest project is Marin (https://marin.community): an openly developed LLM with ten full-time engineers. Pretraining of a 535B MoE with 23B active parameters on 18T tokens was completed on 5. 11. 2026 and could be followed publicly. Discussions with academic laboratories in the USA and Europe about mathematical models are ongoing. Earlier runs used TPUs from Google’s TPU Research Cloud; the Huang Foundation provided the 768 GB200 for the large run. Open Athena also names Lambda, CoreWeave and AWS as partners for compute and infrastructure, and foundations such as Schmidt Sciences and the Sloan Foundation as funders. Germany currently has no comparable organisational layer or support of this kind. In October 2026, Open Athena described why there is no open data commons for post-training (Section 5.3).

Project Numina (Pietro Pellegrino).

Autoformalisation using Lean-4 agents (“Numina Fuse”), public formalisation of the three-dimensional Kakeya conjecture, NuminaMath dataset.

Prover and Formalisation Models.

Goedel-Prover-V2-8B and DeepSeek-Prover-7B (proofs), StepFun-Formalizer-7B (autoformalisation); the platform https://prove2.me for collaborative Lean formalisation.

6.3 Public Computing Infrastructure

JUPITER and GCS.

The first European exascale system at JSC Jülich, 500 million EUR, around 24 000 GH200. The Gauss Centre for Supercomputing allocates half of the compute to German projects (Section 5).

AI Factories.

The 19 EuroHPC AI Factories are the current AI offering for research; the two German locations are HammerHAI (Stuttgart, 55 million EUR, around 860 Blackwell GPUs, second half of 2026) and JAIF (Jülich, 55 million EUR).

AI Gigafactories.

The EU is inviting bids for up to seven industrial-scale data centres, each with 100 000 or more AI accelerators, financed with up to 10 billion EUR in public funding (split equally between the EU and the host Member State), intended to mobilise at least 20 billion EUR in private investment. The original tender deadline (12. 11. 2026) no longer applies. Since 5. 11. 2026, the deadline for Lot 2 has been 3. 12. 2026 and for Lot 1 it has been 4. 2. 2027, because Member States still need to complete their budgetary procedures. Operations are due to begin 18 months after selection, which had previously been scheduled for early 2027. The EU and 18 Member States purchase compute from the operators (joint procurement, including Germany); the tender requires the consortia to guarantee a share of this compute for the EU and the host Member State over the long term. Under Regulation (EU) 2026/150, this time can be used for free access, including research; the share that will actually go to research remains open and subject to political negotiation. The Gigafactories are the industrial tier: with JUPITER, GCS and the AI Factories, research has its own, upstream access. The reported German bidding consortium is Telekom/Schwarz in Lübbenau (up to 100 000 GPUs, approx. 11 billion EUR). This is based on press reporting, rather than confirmed information. SAP has withdrawn.4

GWDG and KISSKI.

GWDG, the computing centre of the University of Göttingen and the Max Planck Society, operates Chat AI with an OpenAI-compatible API together with the KISSKI AI service centre (funded by the BMFTR since November 2022). Members of universities and research institutions can access it through the Academic Cloud. The open-weight models, including GLM-5.3-Flash, DeepSeek V4 Flash, Qwen3.5-397B and Qwen3.8-27B, run in its own data centre, are free of charge and, according to GWDG, do not store inputs. Models from OpenAI and Anthropic are provided through Microsoft Azure and are not free. Other offerings include RAG, a coding assistant, hosting and fine-tuning of users’ own models, and GPU compute. There is therefore already an offering for German research for Horizon 1; for the size of the models operated there, see Section 2.

7 Glossary

Agent, agentic

An LLM that calls tools in a loop (reading files, executing code, searching) and processes intermediate results, rather than simply generating an answer.

Active Parameters

In MoE models, the subset of parameters actually used in computation for each token. This determines speed and training requirements; the total number determines memory requirements. In dense models, all parameters are active (Section 4).

Autoformalisation

Translation of informal mathematical statements and proofs into a formal language such as Lean, so that a kernel can check them. The remaining weak point is the correspondence between the formal and informal statements.

B, T

Billions (billion) and trillions (trillion) of parameters or tokens, respectively.

Distillation

A smaller model learns from the outputs of a larger one. Orders of magnitude cheaper than reinforcement learning.

FLOP, 6NT

Floating-point operations. Training compute is approximately six times the number of (active) parameters multiplied by the number of tokens.

Frontier Model

The most capable models at any given time from the large US companies, currently Claude Fable 5.2 (Anthropic), GPT-6.2 Astra (OpenAI) and Gemini 4 Carbon (Google), as of 17. 11. 2026.

GPU-Hour

One hour of compute on one GPU; the standard measure for training costs. H100: around 3 EUR in rental costs, 1.3 kWh of energy.

Agent Harness

The software surrounding the model: prompts, tools, context management and loop control. A major determinant of agentic performance.

HBM, NVLink

Fast memory directly on the GPU, and the fast connection between GPUs within a node, respectively.

Hyperscaler

An operator of very large data centres that offers compute or model hosting as a service.

Kernel (Lean)

The small, trusted checker that verifies formal proofs. A proof accepted by the kernel is correct; the kernel does not check whether the formal statement corresponds to the intended one.

KV Cache

A cache for each user’s context; it grows linearly with context length and can require more memory than the weights.

MFU

Model FLOPs Utilization: the share of theoretical computing performance actually used during training, typically 30–45 per cent.

MoE

Mixture of Experts: an architecture in which only some of the many subnetworks (experts) are active for each token.

Open Weights, Open Source

Open weights: the weights can be downloaded. Open source in the narrower sense: the data, recipe and code are also open. Most “open” models are only open-weight models.

Post-Training, SFT, RL

The stages after pretraining: supervised fine-tuning on examples (SFT) and reinforcement learning with rewards, for instance through verifiable results.

Pretraining

The initial training on trillions of tokens, the most expensive computational step.

PUE

Power Usage Effectiveness: total electricity consumption of the data centre divided by IT electricity consumption. 1.2 is good; the German average is 1.55.

Quantisation

Storing the weights with fewer bits (4 or 8 rather than 16). Halves or quarters memory requirements with a small loss of quality.

RAG

Retrieval-Augmented Generation: relevant documents from a database are placed in the model’s context, rather than the model having to know them by heart.

Sovereignty

Here: operation or training without dependence on US providers, with data remaining within one’s own organisation or at least within the EU.

TDM, § 60d UrhG

Text and data mining; the copyright exception that allows research institutions to train on copyright-protected texts.

Token

The unit in which models process text, roughly three quarters of a word.
